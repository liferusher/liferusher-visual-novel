# 16. Character Tropes

Tropes are **casting shorthand**. They let a reader orient in thirty seconds,
which is worth an enormous amount in a medium where you meet six people in the
first hour.

They are not characters, and they are not insults. "Tsundere" is a starting
position, the way "baritone" is a starting position for a singer.

## How to read this chapter

Every entry uses the same five fields:

- **The type** — what the reader recognizes
- **Why it works** — the actual function; the reason it survived
- **The failure mode** — how it goes wrong, specifically
- **The load-bearing version** — how to make the trope structural instead of
  decorative
- **A line** — an original example showing the difference

**The one rule that governs all of them:** a trope should be *the shape of a
defense*. Ask what the behavior protects the character from. A tsundere who is
hostile because she's a tsundere is decoration. A tsundere who is hostile
because being liked has historically cost her something is a character.

# Heroine archetypes

## Tsundere

**The type.** Hostile, prickly, or dismissive on the surface; affectionate
underneath. Denies the affection loudly.

**Why it works.** It gives the romance an *obstacle that is also the reward* —
every scene where the armor slips is a beat, so you get twenty small payoffs
instead of one big one. It also makes affection feel earned rather than granted.

**The failure mode.** Mood-swinging with no cause. Hostility that reads as
contempt rather than deflection. Violence played as comedy. The "b-baka!"
delivery, which telegraphs the trope so hard that the reader gets the
information without the pleasure of noticing it.

**The load-bearing version.** Give the armor a *reason* and a *cost*. Then let
the narration supply the evidence of care while the dialogue denies it — the
gap is the entire pleasure (see `docs/02`).

```
Bad:
    "I-it's not like I made this for you or anything, baka!"

Good:
    "I made too much. If you don't take it, it goes in the bin."
    >
    She's already holding the box out. She made this at six in the morning.
    >
    "...It goes in the bin," she repeats, louder.
```

## Kuudere

**The type.** Flat affect, minimal expression, cool and analytical. Warmth is
rare and therefore enormous.

**Why it works.** It creates the highest-contrast emotional payoff in the
medium's toolkit. A kuudere smiling is worth a genki character's entire route,
because you've spent fifteen hours establishing the baseline.

**The failure mode.** Flat becomes *empty*. If a character has no interior life,
"reserved" is indistinguishable from "unwritten". Also: the reveal that she was
Secretly Emotional All Along, delivered as a single tearful scene, which spends
the whole account at once.

**The load-bearing version.** Keep the *interiority* rich even when the affect
is flat. In her POV scenes, or in what she does rather than says, show a person
who feels everything and has decided not to broadcast it. And spend the warmth
in *increments* — a fractional expression change, noted by the narration.

```
    "It's fine."
    >
    Two words. Same as always.
    >
    But she says them to me and not to the window, which is new.
```

## Dandere

**The type.** Shy, quiet, socially anxious. Opens up gradually to one person.

**Why it works.** It maps the romance arc directly onto a visible behavioral
change, so the reader can *measure* progress. Enormously satisfying.

**The failure mode.** Shyness as the entire personality. Also: the "fixed by
love" arc, where the protagonist's attention cures a genuine anxiety disorder,
which is both bad craft and slightly insulting.

**The load-bearing version.** Give her a domain where she is *not* shy — a
subject, a skill, an online persona, a younger sibling she's fierce about. The
contrast makes her a person rather than a symptom, and it gives you scenes.

```
    Three words in an hour, all of them "sorry."
    >
    Then someone asks her about the aquarium's filtration system.
    >
    Eleven minutes. No pauses. I have learned more about nitrogen cycles
    than I intended to learn in my life.
```

## Yandere

**The type.** Affection that curdles into obsession, possessiveness, and
sometimes violence.

**Why it works.** It weaponizes the medium's core promise. VNs are built on
devotion and being chosen; the yandere is that promise turned into a threat.
When it works, it's genuinely frightening in a way other media struggle with,
because the reader has been enjoying devotion for hours.

**The failure mode.** Played entirely for titillation, with the violence as a
quirk. Also: the yandere reveal as a pure twist, with no earlier reading — a
good yandere route should be re-readable, with the early scenes turning sinister
in retrospect.

**The load-bearing version.** Write the early scenes so they read as *sweet* on
pass one and *alarming* on pass two, with nothing changed. That's the craft. If
you're handling self-harm, stalking, or abuse, treat them as real rather than
aesthetic, and warn for them.

```
    "I checked your route home. The one by the river's four minutes longer
    but there's more light."
    >
    That's thoughtful.
    >
    That's a thoughtful thing to have done.
```

## Genki

**The type.** High energy, loud, relentlessly upbeat, drags everyone into things.

**Why it works.** Pure narrative fuel. A genki character *causes scenes* — she
generates plot in a genre that otherwise struggles to make things happen at a
school. She's also the reason the cast is a cast rather than five people who
don't talk.

**The failure mode.** Exhausting. Volume without interiority. And the near-
universal cliché of the "sad genki" reveal, where her cheer is a mask — which is
fine, but so standard now that it lands as a checkbox unless you do something
with it.

**The load-bearing version.** Let the energy be *real* rather than a mask, and
find the cost elsewhere: she's tiring to be around, she doesn't know how to be
quiet with someone, she reads a room badly and knows it. That's fresher than the
hidden-sadness version, and sadder.

```
    "You don't have to do the voice," I tell her.
    >
    "What voice?"
    >
    "The one you're doing."
    >
    She's quiet for exactly as long as it takes to decide not to be.
```

## Ojou-sama

**The type.** Wealthy, refined, often haughty. Sheltered. Formal speech.

**Why it works.** Instant comedy engine (fish-out-of-water with anything
mundane) and instant conflict engine (family obligation vs. personal want).

**The failure mode.** Rich-girl jokes with no other content. The convenience
store scene where she doesn't know what a rice ball is, played four times.

**The load-bearing version.** Make the obligation *real* and specific — a
marriage arrangement, a company, a parent's illness — so her want has a
structural obstacle rather than a personality one.

## Onee-san

**The type.** Older, confident, teasing, protective. Frequently the one who sees
through the protagonist.

**Why it works.** She can *say the true thing* that peer characters can't, which
makes her invaluable for pacing — she moves scenes that would otherwise stall.

**The failure mode.** Teasing with no vulnerability. She becomes a device that
dispenses insight rather than someone with her own want.

**The load-bearing version.** Give her something she is *bad* at, ideally the
thing she coaches others on. The mentor who can't take her own advice is one of
the most reliably moving characters in fiction.

## Osananajimi (childhood friend)

**The type.** Known the protagonist forever. Comfortable, familiar, often loses.

**Why it works.** She comes pre-loaded with history, which means her scenes can
skip the introduction phase entirely and operate at a depth new characters take
ten hours to reach.

**The failure mode.** History *told* rather than *demonstrated*. Flashback dumps
about a promise made under a tree. Also the "childhood friend always loses"
convention played straight, which readers now see coming.

**The load-bearing version.** Demonstrate history through **shorthand** — the
things they don't have to say. That's the whole trope in one technique.

```
Bad:
    "Remember when we were kids and we made that promise at the shrine?"

Good:
    "You're doing the thing."
    "I'm not doing the thing."
    "You've done the thing twice since we sat down."
    >
    I put my hand back on the table.
```

## Kouhai / senpai

**The type.** A junior who looks up to the protagonist; or a senior the
protagonist looks up to.

**Why it works.** Built-in asymmetry. Every line carries a power differential
without you having to write one.

**The failure mode.** Admiration as the entire characterization. The kouhai who
exists to praise.

**The load-bearing version.** Let the junior be *better* at the thing, and let
both of them know it. The senpai's performance of competence becomes the
character.

## The class representative (iinchou)

**The type.** Responsible, organized, rule-enforcing, secretly overextended.

**Why it works.** She's the friction character — a built-in reason for a scene to
have an obstacle in it. Also the natural bridge between the protagonist and any
school-level plot.

**The failure mode.** Nagging as personality.

**The load-bearing version.** Make the responsibility *chosen* and understand why
she chose it. Someone who takes on everything is usually avoiding something.

## The transfer student

**The type.** Arrives from outside. Mysterious, or simply new.

**Why it works.** It's the cleanest way to make a static setting move. Everything
must be explained to her, so exposition becomes natural. And she has a *before*
the reader doesn't know about.

**The failure mode.** The mystery is the only trait, and the answer arrives too
late to be a person.

**The load-bearing version.** Give her an opinion about the place on day one.
Outsiders notice things insiders can't — that's the actual value, and it's free
characterization for your setting as well as for her.

## The ill or hospitalized girl

**The type.** Chronically or terminally ill. Often serene about it.

**Why it works.** It's the medium's most direct route to the nakige payoff, and
it makes ordinary things — a walk, a meal outdoors — into events.

**The failure mode.** Illness as a plot device and a personality. The saintly
patient with no anger in her. And the ending where death is *beautiful*, which is
sentimental rather than moving.

**The load-bearing version.** Let her be **bored, angry, and bad at it.** Real
illness is tedious and undignified and makes people short-tempered. A patient who
is sick of being a patient is worth ten serene ones.

```
    "Everyone does the voice."
    >
    "What voice?"
    >
    "The soft one. Like I'm going to break if you talk normally."
    >
    "...Sorry."
    >
    "You did it again."
```

## The non-human girl

**The type.** Ghost, spirit, android, alien, goddess, animal-eared. Often only
the protagonist can see or understand her.

**Why it works.** Loneliness made literal, plus a built-in tragic clock (she must
leave / she isn't real / she'll be forgotten).

**The failure mode.** The premise does all the work. Being a ghost is a
situation, not a character.

**The load-bearing version.** Decide what she wants that has nothing to do with
her supernatural status, and make the supernatural part *inconvenient* to it.

## Others, briefly

| Type | The reason it works | The failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| **Dojikko** (clumsy) | Endearing, generates small events | Clumsiness as the whole character |
| **Tennen** (airhead) | Says the true thing by accident | Stupidity played for contempt |
| **Gyaru** | Judged-on-sight; subversion is built in | The subversion IS the character |
| **Bookworm** | Interiority is plausible; quiet scenes | Reads books; that's it |
| **Delinquent** | Reputation vs. reality gap | Secretly nice, and nothing else |
| **Idol** | Public face vs. private self, structurally | Fame as substitute for want |
| **Shrine maiden** | Ties setting, folklore, and duty together | Aesthetic only |
| **Maid** | Service as a way of hiding | Service as a fetish with no person |
| **Rival heroine** | Externalizes the protagonist's choice | Exists to be beaten |

# Protagonist archetypes

## The everyman

**The type.** Ordinary, unremarkable, reactive.

**Why it works.** Low barrier. The reader slides in easily, and the cast can be
extraordinary by contrast.

**The failure mode.** Ordinary becomes *blank* (see `docs/06`). A protagonist
with no opinions is not relatable; he's absent.

**The load-bearing version.** Ordinary *circumstances*, specific *person*. He can
have no special skills and still have a strong opinion about instant coffee, a
grudge, and a joke he keeps making.

**Minimum viable protagonist: one strong opinion, one fear, one running joke.**

## The deadpan narrator

**The type.** Dry, sarcastic, comments on everything. The medium's most common
protagonist voice.

**Why it works.** It solves the "nothing is happening" problem — the narration
is entertaining even when the plot isn't. It also makes the eventual sincerity
land, because sincerity costs him something.

**The failure mode.** Snark as a defense against ever writing a real feeling. If
the narrator undercuts every emotional moment, the reader stops offering them.

**The load-bearing version.** Establish that the deadpan is a *choice*, and break
it deliberately, rarely, at maximum cost. The joke he doesn't make is the
loudest line in the route.

## The chuuni

**The type.** Grandiose persona, invented titles, theatrical delivery.

**Why it works.** Comedy that becomes tragedy. The persona is a coping mechanism
and everyone can see it, which makes it funny and then makes it sad.

**The failure mode.** The bit never breaks, or breaks so often it isn't a bit.

**The load-bearing version.** Let the persona *fail* under real pressure — the
moment the character can't perform, and has to speak plainly, is the route.

## The too-nice protagonist

**The type.** Helps everyone, refuses nothing, has no boundaries.

**Why it works.** Generates plot (he'll say yes to anything).

**The failure mode.** Niceness with no cost is not virtue, it's an absence of
character.

**The load-bearing version.** Treat the helpfulness as the pathology it usually
is. Someone who cannot say no is someone who is afraid of something.

## The best friend

**The type.** Comic relief. Loud, crude, loyal, uninvolved in the romance.

**Why it works.** He can voice what the protagonist won't. He's the reason
exposition can happen conversationally. He's also, structurally, the reader's
proxy for "just talk to her."

**The failure mode.** Exists only to set up jokes and disappears when the plot
gets serious — which teaches the reader he was never real.

**The load-bearing version.** Give him one scene where he is the most perceptive
person in the story, and one thing going on in his own life that the protagonist
is too self-absorbed to notice. Cheap to write, disproportionately effective.

## Combining tropes

The most efficient character design move in the medium: **take one archetype and
one contradiction.**

- A genki girl who is *bad* at being around people one-on-one
- A kuudere who is *loud* about exactly one subject
- An ojou-sama with *no money left*, maintaining the performance
- A yandere whose obsession is with the *friendship*, not the romance
- A childhood friend who barely remembers the childhood the protagonist built
  his whole self-image on

The contradiction is where the character lives. The archetype is just how the
reader finds the door.

## The audit

For each named character, answer:

1. What archetype will readers file them under in scene one?
2. What does that behavior *protect them from*?
3. What do they want that the archetype makes harder to get?
4. Where in the story does the armor come off, and what does it cost?
5. What's the contradiction — the thing that isn't in the archetype?

If 2 through 5 are blank, you have casting, not a character.
