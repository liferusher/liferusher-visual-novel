# 11. Scene Construction

How to build a scene from nothing, in order.

## Before you write a box, answer five questions

1. **Whose scene is it?** (Whose want drives it — often not the protagonist's.)
2. **What does that character want in this scene?** Specific and concrete.
3. **What's in the way?**
4. **What changes by the end?** (The TURN. If nothing changes, you have a
   texture scene — that's allowed, but know it.)
5. **What does the reader know that the characters don't, or vice versa?**

If you can't answer 4, you will write a scene that meanders and you'll feel it
at box 60.

## The shape

```
ANCHOR      Where and when. 1 box. Sometimes 0 if it's obvious.
HOOK        A question, oddity, or joke in the first 1-3 boxes.
GOAL        Establish what someone wants (often implicit in dialogue).
ESCALATION  Attempts, obstacles, back-and-forth. The bulk.
TURN        The change. New info, new feeling, new relationship state.
BUTTON      1-3 boxes. Land it. Usually narration. Often an image.
```

## Worked example

**Premise:** Protagonist needs the club president to sign a form. She's avoiding
him because of something that happened last week.

```
ANCHOR
    Clubroom. Four-fifteen. The good light, right before the sun clears
    the gym roof.

HOOK
    Kaede is at the desk with her back to the door, and she has definitely
    heard me come in.
    >
    Ten seconds. She hasn't turned around.

GOAL (implicit — I want the form signed; really I want to know if we're okay)
    "I need a signature."
    "Leave it on the desk."
    "It needs a date too."
    "...Leave it on the desk."

ESCALATION
    I leave it on the desk.
    >
    I don't leave.
    >
    "Was there something else?"
    "Nope."
    >
    The clock on the wall does its thing. Kaede does her thing, which is
    not turning around.
    >
    "You're still here."
    "I'm supervising."
    "It's a *form*."
    "It's an important form."

TURN
    She turns around.
    >
    She's been crying, and she's not going to pretend she hasn't, and
    she's furious that I made her turn around.
    >
    "...There. Happy?"

BUTTON
    "No," I say, which is the first true thing either of us has said all week.
```

Notice:
- No adverbs on speech
- Narration never restates dialogue
- The physical detail (not turning around) carries the whole conflict
- The turn is a *sprite change* the narration contextualizes
- The button is short and lands on a phrase, not an explanation

## Scene types and their shapes

### The introduction scene (new character)
Establish, in order: **how they enter a room**, **what they want**, **what's odd
about them**. Give them one line that only they would say. Do NOT front-load
backstory.

```
Weak:  "Hi! I'm Aoi Fujimoto, I'm 17, I'm in class 2-B, and I love baking!"
Strong: The girl in the doorway is holding a cake tin with both hands and
        looking at it like it's about to go off.
        >
        "Does anyone here know what 'fold gently' means."
        >
        It's not a question.
```

### The confession scene
Structure: **avoidance → the almost → the interruption → the actual thing →
the response → the after.**

The most common failure is length. Hesitate *twice*, then say it. Put the weight
in the **after** — the two minutes following, where nobody knows what to do with
their hands. That's the part readers remember.

### The argument scene
- Both characters must be *right about something*
- Escalate by changing the subject to older grievances
- Someone should say the unforgivable true thing
- End on aftermath, not on the loudest line

### The exposition scene
Attach to want (ch. 6). Break with interruption, disagreement, a joke every 4–5
boxes. Offload optional depth to TIPS.

### The comedy scene
Pick ONE premise and run it. Escalate in threes. End on the biggest version,
then cut immediately — don't wind down.

### The quiet scene (the underrated one)
Two characters, low stakes, mundane activity, no plot. Nakige runs on these.
Requirements: specific activity, comfortable rhythm, ONE small thing that
shifts. These are the scenes readers cite as their favorites.

## The scene-goal grid

For planning a chapter, fill this in per scene before writing:

| # | Location | POV | Whose want | Obstacle | Turn | Button image |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Clubroom | Me | Get the form signed / get forgiven | Kaede's avoidance | She lets me see she's been crying | "No," I say |

If a row's Turn column is empty, you'll find out at draft time. Better to find
out now.

## Openings and endings of the whole work

### First five minutes
Must establish: **voice**, **a question**, and **a reason to like someone**.
Not the world. Not the rules. Not the backstory. Voice, question, affection.

### Last five minutes
Plot answer → emotional answer → image (ch. 6, #9). And then *stop*. The single
most common ending flaw is three extra scenes after the real ending.
