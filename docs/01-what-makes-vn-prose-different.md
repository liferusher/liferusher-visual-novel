# 1. What Makes VN Prose Different

Most bad VN writing is competent novel writing pasted into the wrong container.
Before any style advice, understand the container.

## The five constraints

### 1. The reader controls the clock, one box at a time

A novel reader's eye flows across a page and sets its own rhythm. A VN reader
clicks. Every click is a beat — a hard stop you imposed on them, whether you
meant to or not.

Consequence: **your line breaks are punctuation.** A box that ends mid-thought
creates suspense. A box that ends on a complete thought creates a landing. You
are choosing this every 40-80 characters, hundreds of times per hour.

```
Bad (novel habit — one box, no beats):
    She turned around slowly, and when I saw her face I realized that she had
    been crying the entire time, which explained why she hadn't spoken since we
    left the station.

Better (three boxes, three beats):
    She turned around. Slowly.
    >
    Her face was wet.
    >
    ...So that's why she hadn't said a word since the station.
```

The `>` is the click. Read them aloud with a pause at each one. The second
version has a discovery structure; the first has a summary structure.

### 2. The image is already doing work

There is a sprite on screen with an expression. There is a background. There is
music with a mood. Every word you spend re-describing those is a word the reader
pays for twice.

```
Bad:  Yuki smiled happily. "I'm so glad you came!"     <- sprite is already smiling
Good: "I'm so glad you came!"                          <- let the sprite act
Good: Yuki smiled. It didn't reach her eyes.           <- narration CONTRADICTS or
                                                          COMPLICATES the sprite
```

**Rule: narration should add information the visuals cannot carry** — interior
state, subtext, history, contradiction, the things happening off-screen or
inside the POV character's head.

### 3. Reading speed is high, retention is low

Players read fast, often with auto-advance on, often across sessions weeks
apart. Complex sentence architecture collapses. Nested clauses collapse. Names
you mentioned once three hours ago collapse.

Practical rules:
- One idea per box, two at most.
- Re-anchor names and relationships periodically ("Kaede — the club president,
  the one who'd dragged me into this").
- Put the important word at the end of the box. That's where the reader's eye
  stops before clicking.

### 4. Repetition is structural, not accidental

VNs are long (10–80+ hours) and branch. Readers re-read common routes. Callback
phrases, verbal tics, and repeated framings are how you build the emotional
payoff that VNs are famous for.

A line like a character's catchphrase, a recurring image, or a repeated question
becomes a *load-bearing* device. When it changes in Act 3, the change carries
enormous weight because it's the fiftieth time the reader has seen it.

This is the single biggest craft difference from prose fiction, where repetition
usually reads as weak editing.

### 5. The narrator is usually a character, not a camera

Most VNs are first person, present tense, from inside a protagonist who is
opinionated, funny, wrong about things, and constantly reacting. The narration
*is* a performance. A flat, neutral narrator is the most common reason a VN
feels lifeless in its first hour.

## The format's text categories

Everything you write falls into one of these. Know which one you're in:

| Category | Function | Typical length |
|---|---|---|
| **Dialogue** | Characterization, plot, conflict | 1–3 boxes |
| **Narration** | Interiority, subtext, transition, comedy | 1–2 boxes |
| **Monologue (ADV)** | Protagonist's running commentary | 1 box, frequent |
| **Description** | Establishing a new place/person/object | 2–4 boxes, rare |
| **Choice text** | Player agency | 1 line each, 2–4 options |
| **System/UI text** | Chapter titles, date stamps, tips | minimal |

If a box doesn't clearly belong to one of these, it's usually cuttable.

## ADV vs NVL — pick one and know why

- **ADV** — text box at the bottom, sprites above. The default. Fast, punchy,
  dialogue-forward. Favors short narration and rapid back-and-forth.
  Examples: *Clannad*, *Doki Doki Literature Club*, *Katawa Shoujo*, *Steins;Gate*.
- **NVL** — text fills most of the screen. Favors longer prose blocks, denser
  description, literary register, heavy interiority.
  Examples: *Umineko no Naku Koro ni*, *Fate/stay night* (mixed), *Saya no Uta*.

Many VNs mix: ADV for conversation, NVL for a big interior or expository
sequence. The switch itself is a tonal signal — use it deliberately, not
randomly.

**Wording implication:** NVL tolerates a semicolon. ADV mostly does not.
