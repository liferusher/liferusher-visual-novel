# 5. Good vs Bad Wording

Sentence-level. Each entry: the pattern, why it fails, the fix.

---

## 1. Filter words (the biggest single win)

Filter words put a layer of perception between the reader and the event: *saw,
heard, felt, noticed, realized, watched, seemed, decided, thought, found
myself*.

```
Bad:  I saw her standing by the window.
Good: She's standing by the window.

Bad:  I felt a chill run down my spine.
Good: A chill runs down my spine.
Best: The back of my neck goes cold.

Bad:  I noticed that the door was open.
Good: The door is open.

Bad:  I realized she had been crying.
Good: Her eyes are red. She's been crying.
```

We're in her head already — the POV is implicit. Filtering is redundant *and*
it slows the sentence.

**Exception:** keep the filter when the *act of perceiving* is the point.
`I don't see her at first.` / `It takes me a second to realize what I'm looking at.`

---

## 2. Adverb crutches

```
Bad:  "Get out," she said angrily.
Good: "Get out."

Bad:  He smiled sadly.
Good: He smiled. It was the worst thing he could have done.

Bad:  She walked slowly into the room.
Good: She takes her time coming in.
```

Adverbs on speech verbs are almost always the sprite's job or the line's job.
If the line doesn't sound angry on its own, rewrite the line, not the tag.

---

## 3. Purple prose

```
Bad:  Her cerulean orbs sparkled like the shimmering azure depths of an
      endless summer sea as a single crystalline tear traced its way down
      the porcelain canvas of her delicate cheek.

Good: Her eyes are wet. She won't look away from me.
```

The tell is **stacked ornamental modifiers** and **elegant variation for body
parts** (orbs, locks, tresses, digits). Eyes are eyes. Hair is hair.

Purple prose is not "descriptive writing" — descriptive writing is specific.
Purple prose is vague in an expensive font.

```
Vague-fancy:  a beautiful girl of ethereal grace
Specific:     a girl who holds her bag with both hands like it might escape
```

---

## 4. Redundancy with the visuals

```
Bad:  Yuki entered the room wearing her school uniform, her long brown hair
      tied back in a ponytail. She had a worried expression on her face.
Good: Yuki comes in without knocking. That's new.
```

If the sprite shows it, don't write it. Spend the box on what the sprite can't do.

---

## 5. Stage direction as prose

```
Bad:  I turned my head to the left and looked at Kaede. Then I stood up from
      my chair and walked over to where she was sitting and sat down beside her.
Good: I sit down next to her.
```

Readers auto-fill body mechanics. Write the *meaningful* movement only.

**Keep:** movements that carry meaning.
```
She sits down. Leaves one chair between us.
```

---

## 6. Emotion naming instead of emotion evidence

```
Bad:  I was very sad. It made me feel terrible.
Good: I finish the rest of the bento. It doesn't taste like anything.

Bad:  She was extremely angry.
Good: She sets the cup down. Gently. Which is how I know.
```

Naming an emotion tells the reader what to feel, which makes them resist.
Evidence lets them arrive at it, which makes them own it.

**Exception:** VN narrators *are* allowed to name their own feelings sometimes —
it's first-person interiority, and constant indirection gets precious. The rule
is: earn the naming with evidence first, then name it flat for impact.

```
    She sets the cup down. Gently.
    >
    I have never been this frightened of anyone.
```

---

## 7. Weak intensifiers

```
very, really, quite, rather, somewhat, extremely, totally, literally, actually,
just, basically, kind of, sort of, a bit
```

```
Bad:  I was really very nervous.
Good: My hands won't stay still.

Bad:  It was extremely loud.
Good: I can't hear my own voice.
```

**Exception:** these are excellent *in dialogue* as character fingerprint. An
anxious character should say "sort of" and "I guess" constantly. Kill them in
narration, keep them in voice.

---

## 8. Passive voice where the actor matters

```
Bad:  The letter was found by me on the desk.
Good: The letter is on my desk.

Bad:  Mistakes were made.
Good: I screwed up.
```

**Exception:** passive is correct when the actor is unknown or the point is the
victim's helplessness. `The window had been broken from the outside.` is fine —
that's a mystery sentence doing mystery work.

---

## 9. Talking heads with no grounding

Thirty lines of dialogue with no narration is disorienting — readers lose the
room.

```
Every 6-10 lines, drop one grounding box:
    - a physical action ("She peels the label off her bottle.")
    - a sensory detail ("The AC kicks on.")
    - a time marker ("It's fully dark outside now.")
    - an interior reaction
```

These are called *beats*. They also give you pacing control for free.

---

## 10. Echo and repetition (the accidental kind)

```
Bad:  I looked at her. She looked back at me. I looked away.
Good: I look at her. She doesn't look away. I do.

Bad:  The room was small. The desk in the room was small too.
Good: The room's barely bigger than the desk in it.
```

Scan for the same verb/noun within three boxes. Intentional repetition is a
motif; accidental repetition is a stutter. The difference is whether it's doing
work.

---

## 11. Explaining the joke / explaining the feeling

```
Bad:  "I'm a professional ghost."
      She said something ridiculous, which was funny because ghosts aren't
      a profession.

Good: "I'm a professional ghost."
      "That's not a job."
```

```
Bad:  She smiled at me, and in that moment I understood that she had
      forgiven me and that everything was going to be all right between us
      from now on.

Good: She smiles.
      >
      Okay.
      >
      Okay.
```

Trust the reader. VN readers are, as an audience, extremely willing to feel
things — you do not need to push.

---

## 12. Dialogue that's too grammatical

Real speech fragments, restarts, and trails off.

```
Bad:  "I do not believe that I will be able to attend the festival because
      I have a prior engagement with my family."

Good: "I can't come. Family thing."

Good (anxious character):
      "Ah— sorry, I can't. There's a... my family's doing a thing, so."
```

**Exception:** formal characters should be grammatical. That's the fingerprint.
The rule is *deliberate* grammar, not *default* grammar.

---

## 13. Melodrama without foundation

```
Bad:  "I'll never forgive you! You've destroyed everything I ever cared about!"
      (in chapter 2, over a lost umbrella)
```

Escalation must be earned by prior investment. If a character screams in hour 2,
you have nothing left for hour 30. **Keep your volume ceiling high by keeping
your baseline low.**

The medium's most famous emotional payoffs work because the preceding 15 hours
were mundane, warm, and quiet.

---

## 14. The "..." overdose

```
Bad:  "I... don't know... what to say... about that..."
Good: "I don't know what to say."
      "..."
      "...Sorry."
```

One ellipsis per line, maximum. A line that is *only* `"..."` is a strong beat;
ellipses sprinkled inside every line just makes everyone sound sedated.

---

## 15. Present-tense verbs that don't work in present tense

```
Awkward: I am walking to school, and I am thinking about yesterday.
Better:  I walk to school. Yesterday won't leave me alone.
```

Present-tense narration works best in **simple present**, not present
continuous. Continuous is for genuinely ongoing background action.

---

## Quick reference: search-and-destroy list

Run these searches on any finished draft.

```
\bI (saw|heard|felt|noticed|realized|watched|thought|decided)\b
\b(very|really|quite|extremely|totally|literally|basically|somewhat)\b
\b(suddenly|slowly|quickly|angrily|sadly|happily|nervously)\b
\b(orbs|tresses|locks|porcelain|cerulean|azure|crimson)\b
\bbegan to\b|\bstarted to\b
\bcould (see|hear|feel)\b
\bin that moment\b|\blittle did (I|he|she|they)\b
```

Not every hit is wrong. Every hit deserves ten seconds of thought.
