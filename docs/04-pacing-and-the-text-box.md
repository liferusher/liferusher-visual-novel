# 4. Pacing and the Text Box

This is the most medium-specific chapter and the one most novel writers skip.

## The unit of composition is the box, not the paragraph

Think of your script as a sequence of *cards*, each revealed by a click. Ask of
every card: **what does this one card do?**

A card can:
- deliver one image
- deliver one line of speech
- deliver one thought
- land one joke
- turn the scene
- withhold (end mid-sentence to force the next click)

A card that does none of these is dead weight and the reader feels it as
"padding" even if they can't articulate it.

## Length targets

These are guidelines, not laws. Measure in your actual engine at your actual
font size.

| Text type | Target | Hard ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| ADV dialogue box | 1–2 lines | 3 lines |
| ADV narration box | 1–3 lines | 3 lines |
| NVL block | 4–8 lines | fill screen, never scroll |
| Choice option | 3–9 words | one line, no wrap |
| Chapter/scene title | 2–6 words | one line |

**Never make the reader scroll a text box or click through a wrapped choice.**

## Withholding: the mid-sentence break

The strongest pacing tool in the medium. End a box before the sentence resolves,
and the click becomes anticipation.

```
        When I finally opened the door, what I saw was
        >
        nothing.
        >
        The room was completely empty.
```

Use for: reveals, punchlines, horror, dramatic irony.

**Do not use it constantly.** If every box withholds, nothing withholds. A
reasonable frequency is a handful per scene, at the moments you want the reader
to *feel* the click.

## Cadence patterns

### Accelerate into action
Shorten boxes as tension rises. Long → medium → short → very short.

```
    The hallway stretches ahead of me, lit by the emergency strips along the
    floor, and somewhere far off a door is banging in the wind.
    >
    I take a step. Then another.
    >
    Something moves.
    >
    Close.
    >
    Behind me.
```

### Decelerate into emotion
Lengthen boxes as a scene settles into feeling. The rhythm itself signals "stop
bracing, start feeling."

### The three-beat landing
Setup box → turn box → landing box. Most VN scenes end on this shape.

```
    "I'll be here when you get back."                 <- setup
    >
    She says it like a fact, not a promise.            <- turn
    >
    ...I decide to believe her anyway.                 <- landing
```

## Scene-level pacing

A typical VN "scene" runs 5–20 minutes of reading. Structure that actually holds:

```
1. LOCATION/TIME ANCHOR     1 box. Where, when. Cheap and essential.
2. HOOK                     1-3 boxes. A question, an oddity, a joke.
3. DEVELOPMENT              The body. Dialogue-dominant.
4. TURN                     Something changes — info, mood, relationship.
5. BUTTON                   1-3 boxes. Land it. Often narration.
```

If a scene has no TURN, it is a filler scene. VNs can afford *some* filler
(slice-of-life is built from it) but filler needs to be doing character work or
comedy or both, or it's just length.

## Transitions

VNs transition constantly (scene jumps, time skips, location changes). Wording
conventions that work:

```
Time skip, small:
    ——— Lunch break. ———
    Twenty minutes later, nothing has improved.

Time skip, large:
    Three weeks pass.
    Nothing important happens. That's not a complaint.

Location:
    The walk home takes eleven minutes. I take twenty.

Hard cut (use a black screen / SFX):
    [black]
    ...
    [fade in: hospital room]
```

**Do not narrate travel unless something happens on it.** "I walked to school"
is almost never worth a box; "I walked to school. She wasn't at the corner where
she always waits." is worth three.

## Sound, silence, and the engine

Your text interacts with BGM, SFX, and screen effects. Write for that.

- **Cut BGM before a line, not after.** Silence hitting one beat before the
  important line is the cheapest, strongest dramatic effect in the medium.
- **Don't describe a sound you're also playing.** If there's a thunder SFX,
  don't write "Thunder crashed." Write what it did to the character.
- **Screen shake / flash replaces adverbs.** Let the engine do "suddenly."

```
Bad:   Suddenly, there was a loud crash!
Good:  [SFX: crash] [screen shake]
       My tea is on the floor. I don't remember dropping it.
```

## Auto-mode and voice acting

If you have voice acting, narration boxes are the only unvoiced text — they'll
read *faster* than voiced dialogue. Long narration between voiced lines breaks
rhythm in auto mode. Keep narration tight in voiced sections.

If you have no voice acting, you carry 100% of the performance in wording. That
means more tics, more rhythm variation, more punctuation work.

## A concrete test

Take any finished scene. Click through it at reading speed with a metronome
going. Any place you feel bored, the reader feels it twice. Any place you
clicked three times without learning anything, cut two of those boxes.
