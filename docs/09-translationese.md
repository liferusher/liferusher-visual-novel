# 9. Translationese — Writing English That Doesn't Read Like a Fan Patch

Most English-language VN writers learned the medium from translated Japanese
work. That means they absorbed not just Japanese *style* but the artifacts of
translation. Some of those artifacts are charming conventions of the genre;
others are just awkward English.

This chapter separates them.

## Conventions that are FINE to keep (they're genre dialect now)

- **Honorifics** (-san, -kun, -chan, -senpai) in a Japanese-set story. Readers of
  the genre parse them fluently and they do real work (see ch. 2).
- **Sound-effect text** in narration: "The door slides open with a *clack*."
- **`「」`-style quoting** if your engine and setting support it aesthetically.
- **Onomatopoeia for internal states** used sparingly (*doki doki* for a racing
  heart) — but only if your work is otherwise steeped in the idiom.
- **Character-defining verbal tics** including catchphrases and sentence-ending
  particles rendered as English mannerisms.
- **The `...` beat line.** Universal in the medium.

## Artifacts that just read as bad English

### 1. Over-literal pronoun and subject repetition

Japanese drops subjects; translations over-restore them.

```
Awkward: I opened the door. I walked into the room. I looked at the desk.
         I saw that the letter was gone.
Better:  I open the door. The letter's gone from the desk.
```

### 2. "It cannot be helped" / "as expected"

Direct calques (*shikata ga nai*, *sasuga*). They're recognizable, and they're
stiff.

```
Calque:  It can't be helped.
Natural: Nothing to be done about it. / Figures. / Yeah, that's about right.

Calque:  As expected of Yuki-senpai!
Natural: Of course she'd have already done it. / Classic Yuki.
```

### 3. The overloaded "somehow" / "somewhat" / "for some reason"

*Nantonaku* / *nanka* translated literally, everywhere.

```
Awkward: For some reason, I somehow felt somewhat uneasy.
Better:  Something about the room is wrong and I can't name it.
```

### 4. "Eh?" / "Ehh?!" / "Fufu" / "Ara ara" in an English-native work

Fine in a Japan-set story with a translated-feel register. Actively odd in an
English-set contemporary story. Pick a lane.

English equivalents that carry the same beats:
```
Eh?        -> Huh? / What? / Sorry?
Ehhh?!     -> You're kidding. / Wait, WHAT?
Fufu       -> a soft laugh (describe it)
Ara ara    -> "Oh my." / "Well, well." / describe the tone
Itadakimasu-> (no equivalent; describe or omit)
```

### 5. Stiff formal register for no reason

```
Awkward: It is unfortunate, but I am unable to accompany you on this occasion.
Natural: Sorry — can't make it this time.
```

Unless the character IS formal. Then keep it, and make it consistent.

### 6. Adverb-first sentences everywhere

```
Awkward: Slowly, I turned around. Suddenly, she spoke. Quietly, I nodded.
Better:  I turn around. She speaks before I've finished. I nod.
```

### 7. Rhetorical-question narration overload

```
Awkward: What was this feeling? Why did my chest hurt? What did it mean?
         Could this be...?
Better:  My chest hurts. I decide not to examine why.
```

One rhetorical question is a beat. Four in a row is a soap opera.

### 8. "That person" / "that child" as a referent

*Ano hito* / *ano ko* rendered literally reads oddly in English, where we'd use
a name or a pronoun.

### 9. Sentence-final "...you know?" / "...isn't it?" on every line

Particle calques (*ne*, *yo*). One character can have this as a tic. The whole
cast having it is a translation artifact.

## Writing English VNs that feel native

Study English-language VNs that built their own voice rather than imitating a
translation:

- **Katawa Shoujo** — Japan-set, written in English by a Western team; a good
  study in using the setting's conventions without the translation stiffness.
- **Butterfly Soup** — contemporary Californian teen voice, extremely specific
  and unmistakably English-native.
- **VA-11 Hall-A** — heavily stylized, internet-inflected, invented slang.
- **Doki Doki Literature Club** — deliberately uses genre-standard register in
  act 1 and then breaks it; the break only works because act 1 is accurate.
- **Analogue: A Hate Story** — formal, archival, a register of its own.
- **Coffee Talk** — warm, low-key, naturalistic multi-ethnic dialogue.
- **Slay the Princess** — second person, theatrical, voice-driven.

## The test

Read a page aloud. If you hear a **translation** — someone's careful, slightly
stiff English rendering of a livelier original — rewrite it until you hear a
**person**.
